# Negotiation strategy

The savings are not in the spreadsheet. They are in the renewal calendar. Whoever controls the sequence controls the price.

This is the part of the case that most people skip. It is easy to say "consolidate onto Asana and save $610K." It is harder to say how you actually get Asana to that price, in what order you touch the four contracts, and what you do when a vendor says no. That is what this file is for.

## The calendar is the strategy

| Vendor | Expires in | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday.com | 3 months | Migrate its users, let it lapse. Do not renew. |
| Asana | 4 months | Sign the expanded three-year consolidated deal here. |
| Trello | 6 months | Migrate its users, let it lapse. |
| Jira | 9 months | Audit active users, then renew or right-size. |

Monday.com expiring one month before Asana is a gift. It means I can retire a vendor first, in full view of Asana's account team, and walk into the Asana renewal having already proven that Meridian will cut a tool it is not getting value from. That is worth more than any slide. The credible threat to walk is the entire basis of the negotiation, and I get to demonstrate it before I need it.

If the calendar were reversed, with Asana expiring first, I would have far less leverage, because I would be asking Asana to expand my commitment before I had shown any willingness to shrink a vendor. Sequence is leverage.

## The Asana renewal, worked out

This is the deal that matters. Asana goes from 650 purchased seats to 710, absorbs the Monday.com and Trello users, and locks in for three years. In exchange I want the rate down and the terms up.

### What I know going in

- Current list rate is $1,200 per seat per year (their $780,000 over 650 seats).
- I am increasing committed seats and committing for three years. Both are things Asana's sales team is compensated on. Expansion plus term is exactly the shape of deal they want to close.
- Their quarter-end and their fiscal year-end are pressure points I would confirm and time the close against.

### BATNA

My walk-away alternative is real, which is what makes it useful. If Asana will not move, I standardise on Monday.com instead. Monday.com scored 3.20 on the scorecard, it is a competent tool, and its own renewal timing means I could pivot the consolidation to it. It is not my first choice, and Asana does not need to know how much I prefer them. The point is that I am not cornered. My weaker second BATNA is status quo plus aggressive de-licensing: keep Asana at the retained-seat count only, strip the 140 idle seats, and walk away from the expansion. Either alternative leaves me better off than accepting a bad renewal.

### Target, opening, walk-away

| | Rate per seat | On 710 seats |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening ask | $900 | $639,000 |
| Target | $1,000 | $710,000 |
| Walk-away (worst I accept) | $1,100 | $781,000 |

I open at $900 to anchor below target, expecting to settle around $1,000. If I cannot get under $1,100 I stop expanding and fall back to the BATNA, because above that line the consolidation stops paying for itself against the risk of putting everyone on one vendor.

### Concession ladder

I do not give anything away for free. Each thing I concede is traded for something I want. The order matters: I lead with the concessions that cost me little and that Asana values highly.

**What I can give (low cost to me, high value to Asana):**

1. Three-year term instead of one. Predictable revenue is what their finance team wants.
2. Case-study and reference rights. Meridian is a recognisable mobility brand and Asana's marketing wants the logo.
3. Upfront annual payment instead of monthly, if the discount justifies the cash-flow cost.
4. A reasonable expansion floor, committing not to drop below a seat count.

**What I want in return:**

1. Rate at target or below.
2. A dedicated customer success manager, named, not a shared queue.
3. Admin and end-user training for the migrating Monday.com and Trello teams, bundled, not billed.
4. A sandbox environment for testing workflows before rollout.
5. An uptime SLA with service credits that bite.
6. A flex-down clause. This is the one I will not give up, because it is the clause that prevents this whole problem from recurring. I want the right to reduce seats at each anniversary within a band, so that idle licences never build up again.

The flex-down clause is where I would spend my last piece of leverage. Everything else is money. That clause is governance. It is the difference between fixing the problem once and fixing it every year.

## What I would confirm before the first call

- Asana's fiscal year-end and quarter close, to time the negotiation into their pressure window.
- Whether the migrating users genuinely need full Enterprise seats or whether some fit a lower tier, which changes the seat math.
- Reference pricing. What comparable mobility or tech firms of our size actually pay per Asana seat, so my anchor is grounded in market rather than in their list price.

## Honest limits of this plan

I have modelled the negotiated rate, not achieved it. The $1,000 target is a reasoned position from list price, deal shape and standard SaaS discounting on multi-year expansions, not a quote I have in writing. In a live deal the rate would move with how badly Asana wants the quarter, how real my Monday.com BATNA looks to them, and how disciplined I stay on the walk-away. The structure of the strategy holds regardless. The specific number is the part that only a real negotiation settles.
